# Turn the dashboard from mirror into coach

The evidence points one way. A personal health dashboard improves numbers when each metric sits inside a loop of **goal, feedback against the user's own normal, and one next action**, shown in a calm, linear, glanceable form. It does not improve numbers when it shows more charts. Self-monitoring alone is the weakest behaviour-change technique, and pairing it with goal setting, feedback and goal review raised pooled effect sizes from 0.26 to 0.42 across 122 diet and activity interventions ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/)). Oura's current app reflects this. It cut five tabs to three, leads Today with a single prioritised "One Big Thing", and colours every metric against personal baselines rather than raw values ([Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/new-oura-app-experience/); [Instrument](https://www.instrument.com/work/oura-app)). Whoop, Garmin, Apple and Fitbit converge on the same ideas: personal typical-range bands, a curated Today feed, and behaviour-versus-outcome analysis gated on minimum sample sizes. Their failures (Garmin's 2024 loss of density, MyFitnessPal's 2026 Diary removal, score anxiety and orthosomnia) show what to avoid. The existing four-tab dashboard already uses several correct primitives: bullet bars, bedtime-to-wake range bars, with-vs-without diverging bars, and 7-day averages. What it most needs are personal baseline bands, forgiving weekly goals in place of brittle daily streaks, insight cards that show sample size and confounders and lead to a two-week experiment, and a calorie display that uses ranges instead of a running deficit ledger.

## Good dashboards pair every number with a reference and a next step

Stephen Few's definition still holds: a dashboard is a single-screen, at-a-glance monitoring display. His catalogued pitfalls include exceeding one screen, showing excessive precision, and "meaningless variety" in chart types ([The Data School summary of Few](https://www.thedataschool.co.uk/anh-vu/are-you-making-these-13-dashboard-design-mistakes/)). Few designed the **bullet graph** to replace gauges. It shows one measure against a target and muted qualitative bands in a small linear space ([BetterEvaluation](https://betterevaluation.org/node/401)). Tufte's word-sized sparklines add trend context to a tile at almost no cost in space ([Tufte](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/)). Shneiderman's mantra, "overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand", maps cleanly onto the four tabs. Today is the overview, Trends handles zoom and filter, Insights handles the "relate" task, and tapping a tile gives details on demand ([Shneiderman 1996](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/5784)). Apple's charting guidance adds a warning: "Resist the temptation to pack as much data as possible into a chart" ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/charting-data)).

Perception research favours linear encodings. A 92-person crowdsourced study found that a plain 24-hour linear bar chart was "most accurate and efficient and is also preferred by the users" over radial layouts, even for daily periodic patterns ([Waldner et al.](https://cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2019/waldner-2019-rld)). On phones, linear range charts of self-tracked sleep were faster than radial ones at week, month and year scales. Comparing an observed range against a superimposed average range worked well in both layouts ([Brehmer et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/visualizing-ranges-over-time-on-mobile-phones-a-task-based-crowdsourced-evaluation/)). This supports the dashboard's existing bullet bars and bedtime-to-wake bars, and argues against adding rings as the primary encoding. People read partial circle sectors inconsistently, switching between area and length cues ([Kiesel et al.](https://diglib.eg.org/bitstreams/90e4016f-7cd4-45af-a855-809e79eb524a/download)). For colour, blue against orange is the safest colourblind pair, and status should never be carried by colour alone ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/colorblindness-part2)).

Personal-informatics research shows where self-trackers get stuck. Li, Dey and Forlizzi's five-stage model (preparation, collection, integration, reflection, action) shows barriers in early stages cascading into later ones ([Ian Li](https://ianli.owlstown.net/projects/16)). The integration and reflection stages are exactly what a combined MFP + Oura + Garmin dashboard exists to solve. Choe's Visualized Self study found that most tools offer little reflection support "beyond simple feedback". Integrating sources on shared timelines with temporal comparisons helped participants reach many kinds of insight ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/understanding-self-reflection-people-reflect/)). Epstein's lived-informatics model makes **lapsing and resuming a normal phase of tracking**, not an edge case ([Epstein et al. 2015](https://my.eng.utah.edu/~cs5540/au16/readings/PersonalInformatics-Epstein2015.pdf)). Persistent glanceable summaries also matter. In UbiFit Garden, participants with an ambient phone-wallpaper display maintained activity through holiday weeks, while those without it declined significantly ([Consolvo et al. CHI 2008](https://makeabilitylab.cs.washington.edu/media/publications/Consolvo_ActivitySensingInTheWildAFieldTrialOfUbifitGarden_CHI2008.pdf)).

There are two ways to compare a value: against a goal and against the user's own normal. They should look different. A target tick answers "did I hit my plan?" A muted baseline band answers "is this normal for me?" Researchers warn that normative baselines and statistical smoothing can impose assumptions about "normal progress" that clash with lived experience ([Snyder](https://tmilab.colorado.edu/socialpi/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Snyder.pdf)). A 2026 co-design preprint recommends making uncertainty and missing context visible and never presenting observed patterns as causal or clinical conclusions ([arXiv 2609.15046](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.15046)).

## Oura curates one thing and colours everything against your baseline

Oura's redesign replaced five tabs (Home, Readiness, Sleep, Activity, Resilience) with three ([Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/new-oura-app-experience/)). **Today** is a feed "modeled on a news app's front page", with Readiness, Sleep and Activity scores on top and a single highlighted "One Big Thing" ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/10/20/oura-app-redesign/)). **Vitals** is a user-reorderable set of colour-coded metric cards (HRV, resting HR, respiratory rate, temperature deviation), each compared with personal historical baselines over timeframes "from days to years" ([Trusted Reviews](https://www.trustedreviews.com/reviews/oura-ring-5); [Wareable](https://www.wareable.com/wearable-tech/oura-ring-5-review)). **My Health** holds long-term trends, Habits and Routines sections, Stress Management and the Oura Advisor AI ([TechCrunch](https://www.techcrunch.com/2025/10/20/oura-launches-redesigned-app-and-cumulative-stress-feature)).

Sources disagree on timing. TechCrunch dates the three-tab redesign to October 2025, while Wareable ties a redesign to the Ring 4 launch in October 2024. The structure is the same either way. Instrument, the agency behind the redesign, describes three levels of visualisation built on progressive disclosure: abstract rings, bars and colour cues for the glance; focused metric views for short-term patterns; and precise interactive charts for long-term exploration. It named the original problem plainly: data had become hard "to find, interpret, and act on" ([Instrument](https://www.instrument.com/work/oura-app)).

Oura's most transferable idea is its **semantic colour system**. "Color adapts to personalized baselines, signaling how the body is responding rather than simply displaying raw values" ([Shorty Awards](https://shortyawards.com/18th/test-2)). Scores run 0–100 with four bands: **85–100 Optimal, 70–84 Good, 60–69 Fair, 0–59 Pay attention** ([Oura support](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360057791533)). Older blog copy used three bands, which shows that vocabulary drifts. Each score breaks down into contributors with their own bars. Readiness has nine, including HRV balance, body temperature, recovery index, sleep balance and activity balance. Only contributors that need attention show a red bar, and Oura frames each one as a plain question about the user's own normal ("How well did I sleep last night compared to normal?") ([Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/readiness-score/)). Temperature is shown as a deviation from the personal mean, not an absolute value ([openwearables](https://openwearables.io/blog/oura-api-accessing-ring-data-sleep-hrv-readiness)). Daytime stress uses no fixed thresholds: "Your scores are relative to you" ([Oura support](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/21205822135315-Daytime-Stress)). The tone is deliberately non-judgemental. The low band is called "Pay attention", not "poor", and Advisor uses hedged phrasing such as "suggests you might be waking up feeling a bit depleted" ([LinkedIn critique](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/why-oura-needs-rename-its-stress-metric-andy-proctor-xu7vc)).

Oura has weaknesses too. A "stressed" reading that contradicts how a user feels erodes trust ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/why-oura-needs-rename-its-stress-metric-andy-proctor-xu7vc)). The app never says what a typical bpm or ms value looks like ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/smart-rings/oura-ring-5-hands-on-review-ive-worn-the-worlds-smallest-smart-ring-for-a-week-and-it-changes-fitness-tracking-forever)). Scores keep multiplying (Resilience, Daytime Stress, Cumulative Stress), and Oura manages this by curation (One Big Thing) rather than removal. Its visual identity is calm and warm-neutral, with "calming color schemes, and approachable graphics" ([Oura IP notice](https://ouraring.com/intellectual-property-notice)) and a quiet grotesque typeface, reportedly Akkurat ([shadcn.io extraction](https://www.shadcn.io/design/oura)). Exact app colour tokens are not public.

## Competitors converge on baselines, Today feeds and gated correlation insights

The five comparison apps differ in vocabulary but share three patterns. Each frames metrics relative to a personal baseline, consolidates around a Today or daily-briefing surface, and increasingly links behaviours to outcomes. Their missteps are as useful as their features.

| App | Headline structure | "Normal for me" treatment | Behaviour → outcome | Lesson from backlash |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oura | 3 scores + One Big Thing; Today / Vitals / My Health | Baseline-driven colour; temperature as deviation | Habits/Routines in My Health; tags add context but don't change scores | Too many scores; "stress" label mismatch |
| Whoop | Sleep / Recovery / Strain dials; Recovery green ≥67, yellow 34–66, red ≤33 ([WHOOP dev docs](https://developer.whoop.com/docs/whoop-101)) | Strain target set from recovery ([Strain Coach](https://whoop.com/thelocker/strain-coach)) | Journal impact needs ≥5 yes and ≥5 no logs in 90 days, labelled correlation ([WHOOP](https://www.whoop.com/ca/en/thelocker/a-new-way-to-see-insights-on-which-behaviors-affect-your-recovery/)) | Insights hard to find; one-sided samples unreliable ([community](https://community.whoop.com/t/issues-with-insights-impacts-accuracy/929)) |
| Garmin Connect | Many separate metrics; customisable Home cards since 2024 ([Garmin](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/blog/fitness/new-garmin-connect-mobile/)) | HRV Status: 7-day average vs 3-week personal range ([Garmin](https://www.garmin.com/en-PH/blog/fitness/understanding-the-hrv-status-on-your-garmin-smartwatch/)) | Morning Report briefing ([manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals-apac/webhelp/lily2/EN-SG/GUID-B1C347E8-727F-4309-9FB0-27AE2A2D05E5-9742.html)) | Bigger cards meant more scrolling and lost density ([Advnture](https://www.advnture.com/news/garmin-connect-feedback)) |

**Whoop** offers the closest model for the Insights tab. Users log yes/no behaviours in a Journal. Once a behaviour has at least five "yes" and five "no" days in 90 days, the app shows its average association with Recovery and how many more logs are needed before it qualifies. The Monthly Performance Assessment repeats this over up to a year and ranks the most positive and most negative behaviours ([WHOOP MPA](https://whoop.com/eu/en/thelocker/monthly-performance-assessment-now-features-enhanced-recovery-analysis)). Whoop's own staff admit that estimates drawn from one-sided samples are less reliable ([WHOOP community](https://community.whoop.com/t/issues-with-insights-impacts-accuracy/929)), which is a reason to enforce the gate strictly. Whoop's sleep planner also shows how to turn a score into a target. The user picks "Peak, Perform or Get by" (100/85/70% of computed sleep need), and the app sets the bedtime ([WHOOP](https://whoop.com/thelocker/how-much-sleep-do-i-need)).

**Garmin** shows the risk of having no common vocabulary. Body Battery has four bands, Training Readiness five labelled tiers from "Let your body recover" to "Best possible", and HRV Status four labels ([Garmin manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-31D23DBB-57C2-4DF7-A0C9-8D1A00AB4BE7/EN-US/GUID-C21BE0C8-A08E-4DA1-B6C6-2E0E2DDDB372.html)). Garmin's 2024 redesign brings the density lesson. Larger cards drew complaints that condensed data was harder to see at a glance, and reviewers objected to a fixed slot they could not reassign ([DC Rainmaker](https://www.dcrainmaker.com/2024/01/garmin-connect-mobile-revamp-walk-through.html)). Garmin's HRV Status, a 7-day average plotted against a personal range, is the clearest "relative to normal" chart in the category.

**Apple** brings two anti-anxiety patterns worth copying. Vitals labels nights only "Typical" or "Outlier" against a band built from about seven nights, and notifies only when two or more vitals deviate together ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/smartwatches/watchos-11-public-beta-i-tried-the-new-apple-watch-vitals-health-app-heres-how-it-works-and-first-impressions)). Pause Rings, added in watchOS 11, lets users suspend goals without losing streaks, and ring goals can vary by weekday ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/2446605/how-to-pause-apple-watch-activity-rings.html)). Apple's long-window Trends compare 90 days with 365 days, attach concrete coaching such as "Walk an extra quarter of a mile a day", and make clear that one unusual day "won't ruin your trends" ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/233093/ios-13-and-apple-watch-activity-trends-give-you-the-big-picture.html)).

**MyFitnessPal** is a cautionary tale for any nutrition view. Its April 2026 move from Diary to Today reportedly hid macros behind per-meal taps, removed copy-food shortcuts and offered no revert. One market-intelligence source puts the resulting rating drop at 1.7 stars, though only that single source reports the figure ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/articles/myfitnesspal-v26-16-0-replaces-diary-with-new-ui-sparking-rating-drop-in-april-2026)). **Fitbit**'s October 2025 redesign, by contrast, offered a way back to the old UI and shifted emphasis toward weekly trends and an AI coach ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/health-fitness/fitness-apps/fitbits-new-ai-powered-personal-coach-app-is-now-rolling-out-to-some-heres-whats-new)). Across 2025–26 the industry has moved toward a Today feed, AI coaching, weekly framing and paywalls (Garmin Connect+ at $6.99/month, Whoop tiers, Fitbit and Oura subscriptions) ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/health-fitness/garmin-connect-plus)).

## Framing decides whether users improve or just worry

Feedback changes how people feel regardless of what their data actually shows. In a randomised study of 63 people with insomnia, sham "poor sleep" feedback lowered evening alertness (d = 0.79) and raised sleepiness and fatigue, independent of actual sleep ([Gavriloff et al., Oxford Health](https://www.oxfordhealth.nhs.uk/orka/title/sham-sleep-feedback-delivered-via-actigraphy-biases-daytime-symptom-reports-in-people-with-insomnia-implications-for-insomnia-disorder-and-wearable-devices/)). A pilot in healthy sleepers found no such effect, so the risk concentrates in people who already have sleep complaints ([PMC10899903](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10899903/)). Baron's orthosomnia case series describes patients who spent extra time in bed chasing tracker numbers and ended up sleeping less ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27855740/)). Trackers identified deep sleep correctly only 59% of the time in a 2021 study ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-hygiene/orthosomnia)). A Stanford clinician suggests weekly rather than daily review for anyone the data stresses ([Stanford Lifestyle Medicine](https://lifestylemedicine.stanford.edu/?p=46193)).

Goal structure matters as much as wording. Silverman and Barasch's seven studies found that showing an *intact* streak increased later engagement and showing a *broken* one reduced it. This depended only on how the log represented the behaviour, not on the behaviour itself, and the effect weakened when people could repair the streak. The authors advise against calling out broken streaks ([CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/news/2023/04/20/research-streaks-marketing-tech-barasch)). Sharif and Shu found that a hard goal with built-in "emergency reserve" skip days beat both easy and hard goals without slack. In a five-week pedometer field study, the reserves group took up to 20% more steps, because one miss no longer made the goal unattainable ([UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/)). The goal-gradient effect means people accelerate as they near a goal, and a head start (two pre-filled stamps) speeds completion ([Kivetz et al.](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf)). Adaptive step goals based on the user's own recent percentile reduced the decline in steps to less than half that of static 10,000-step goals over four months ([BMC Public Health 2017](https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12889-017-4197-8)). Habits take a median of **66 days** to become automatic, ranging from 18 to 254, and a single missed day does not derail formation ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)).

Correlation insights carry a specific risk: people read their own correlations as causes. Adding an explanation to an inferential result raised causal interpretation by 15.2% ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/218784v1.full)). A simple correlation-versus-causation warning made readers more cautious ([arXiv 2012.14477](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.14477)). The TummyTrials app showed that people need scaffolding to run valid n-of-1 experiments, and that they want flexibility within them ([Karkar et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5432136)).

Nutrition needs the most care. Among 105 people diagnosed with an eating disorder, about 75% used MyFitnessPal, and **73% of those felt it contributed to their disorder** ([PubMed 28843591](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28843591/)). Calorie-tracking use is associated with higher eating-disorder symptoms in college samples, though the evidence is cross-sectional and causation is unproven ([Center for Research](https://www.center4research.org/fitness-tracking-apps-eating-disorders/)). Short-horizon "stop" goals are especially prone to the what-the-hell effect, where one lapse becomes "today is ruined" ([EFPSA](https://more.efpsa.org/rpblog/?p=1172)). Wearables themselves deliver only modest gains: about +1,800 steps/day across an umbrella review of 163,841 participants, mostly from deliberate walking ([Patient Care](https://www.patientcareonline.com/view/physical-activity-trackers-umbrella-analysis-finds-significant-positive-impact-on-walking-weight-loss)). In the IDEA trial, adding a wearable to a weight-loss programme led to *less* weight loss at 24 months ([MedicalXpress](https://m.medicalxpress.com/news/2016-09-trackers-ineffective-sustaining-weight-loss.html)). Personalisation and messaging are the features associated with larger effects ([BJSM 2021 copy](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/147424/1/422.full.pdf)).

## Concrete changes, tab by tab

The current dashboard is well chosen in form. Bullet bars, range bars, diverging bars and 7-day averages all match the perception evidence. The changes below are mostly about **reference frames, forgiveness, evidence quality and action**, not new chart types.

### Today: one focus, two reference frames, no orphan numbers

Start Today with a single prioritised focus card, Oura's One Big Thing pattern, written as an action tied to data. An example: "Readiness below your usual. Keep training easy and aim for bed by 22:45." Demote the other focus suggestions below it, or collapse them. Every goal-progress bullet bar should carry a target tick and muted qualitative bands, as Few specified. Nutrition targets should be shown as **ranges** (a protein band, a calorie band) rather than single numbers, with neutral or amber styling inside and above the band instead of red. Recovery signal tiles (HRV, resting HR, sleep, Oura readiness, Garmin Body Battery) need a personal baseline band, such as a 30-day median with its interquartile range, a word label ("Within your usual", "Lower than usual"), and a 7–14-day sparkline. That follows Oura Vitals, Garmin HRV Status and Apple Vitals. Use one shared three-word band vocabulary across all three data sources rather than importing Garmin's five tiers and Oura's four. Flag an outlier state only when two or more recovery signals deviate together, as Apple Vitals does. Mark today's partial data as in progress, and mark estimated values (AI-estimated meals, for example) with "~". For low scores, use a four-part copy pattern: neutral fact against the user's range, a non-blaming likely factor visible in the data, one small action, and reassurance from the weekly trend. Add a "weekly progress" line ("Protein in range 3 of 5 days this week") so the daily view feeds a forgiving weekly goal. Today should fit one desktop viewport with roughly 5–7 headline items. That number is a heuristic drawn from Few's short-term-memory rationale, not a tested threshold.

### Habits: weekly goals with reserves, forgiving streaks, cues attached

Change daily pass/fail goals to **weekly targets with built-in reserve days** ("bedtime within 30 minutes of target, 5 of 7 nights"). Show progress as a count ("3 of 5") on the consistency grid, and count work already done when a new week starts. Keep streaks only while they are intact, and change their unit to weeks that met target. When a streak breaks, never show a "0" or send an alert. Fall back to a cumulative view ("41 of the last 50 days") and provide a pause or freeze, as Apple's Pause Rings does, for travel and illness. Grey out days with missing data (ring not worn, nothing logged) rather than counting them as misses, and show a neutral "welcome back" state after a lapse. Let the user attach an if-then cue to each habit ("After dinner → log meal"). Implementation intentions have a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)). Show a habit-formation arc ("week 3; typically 2–3 months, varies widely") rather than a finish line. Keep the bedtime-to-wake range bars, which match Brehmer's evidence. Overlay the target window and the user's average range as a band, and colour nights by consistency with the target rather than by sleep score. Base default step and sleep targets on the user's own recent percentile, and let the user override them.

### Insights: diverging bars become evidence cards with an experiment button

The with-vs-without diverging bars are the right chart, but each needs Whoop-style gating and honest uncertainty. Hide or grey out any habit with fewer than about 10 days in either condition, and show "N more days needed" with a progress indicator. The correct threshold is a judgement call; Whoop uses 5 and 5. Add sample sizes on each bar (e.g. "23 vs 41 nights"), a typical-noise band or interval behind the difference so small effects visibly sit inside the noise, and a confidence word (Low / Medium / High) rather than p-values. Write headlines as observations ("After late dinners, next-morning HRV was lower"), never as causes. Add a caveat line naming confounders actually present in the data, such as Garmin training load, logged alcohol and weekends. Rank insights by how controllable the behaviour is times confidence, and pair each with a **"Try for 2 weeks"** button. That button should create a temporary Habits goal and, at the end, report the before/after difference against the noise band, with "inconclusive" as a legitimate result. Keep the week-in-review table, but lead with wins, add one pattern and one goal-adjustment prompt (goal review is one of the control-theory techniques Michie's analysis rewards), and compare with the user's 4-week average rather than with the previous week alone. **Retire the running calorie balance as a cumulative deficit ledger.** It encodes a short-horizon "stop" goal and invites compensatory eating or exercise, and the MFP evidence above shows real risk in vulnerable users. Replace it with a 7-day rolling average intake against a target band. Keep Garmin exercise calories out of food budgets unless the user opts in, and allow calories to be hidden entirely while protein and fibre "add" goals stay visible.

### Trends: aligned small multiples with baselines and honest gaps

Stack each metric family (sleep duration, HRV, resting HR, calories, protein, steps) as aligned small multiples on a shared date axis, each with its own y-scale. Do not use dual-axis charts. Readers then compare sources by vertical alignment, which addresses the integration barrier personal-informatics research identifies. Keep the 7-day averages and goal lines, and add a personal baseline band behind physiological metrics. Draw daily values as faint dots, with the rolling average as the bold line. Break lines at missing days rather than interpolating or zero-filling. Add a 7D / 30D / 90D / 1Y range control, and a long-window comparison in Apple's style (last 90 days vs last year, with a one-sentence coaching nudge) once enough history exists. Default the sleep views to weekly granularity to limit day-to-day score chasing. In recent activities, link each workout to the next morning's recovery signals, so the activity list explains what the recovery tiles show. Use rounded, unit-consistent numbers in tabular figures throughout ("7 h 24 m", "2,140 kcal").

### Cross-cutting

Make every tile tappable to a single detail sheet (two disclosure levels at most) with definition, data source and last sync. Showing provenance builds trust in merged data. Let the user reorder and hide cards, and offer a compact density mode; Garmin's backlash shows dense layouts are valued. Use a colourblind-safe blue/orange pair with redundant words or icons, and reserve red for genuinely abnormal readings such as a multi-signal illness flag. Never change band labels silently after users have learned them, as Apple did with Sleep Score.

| Priority | Change | Tab | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Personal baseline bands + word labels on recovery tiles and trend charts | Today, Trends | Oura Vitals, Garmin HRV Status, Apple Vitals |
| 2 | Weekly goals with reserve days; intact-only weekly streaks with pause | Habits | Sharif & Shu; Silverman & Barasch; Pause Rings |
| 3 | Sample-size gate, noise band, confounders and "Try 2 weeks" on diverging bars | Insights | Whoop Journal; TummyTrials; causal-bias studies |
| 4 | Replace running calorie balance with 7-day average vs range; calories hideable | Insights, Today | Levinson et al.; what-the-hell effect |
| 5 | Single prioritised focus card with supportive low-score copy | Today | Oura One Big Thing; Gavriloff sham feedback |
| 6 | Gaps shown as gaps; welcome-back state; no zero-filling | All | Epstein lived informatics |
| 7 | Aligned small multiples; density toggle; tap-to-detail with source | Trends, all | Few; Shneiderman; Garmin backlash |

## Conclusion

A combined MFP + Oura + Garmin dashboard can offer something none of the source apps do: it can test the user's own food, sleep and training behaviours against next-morning physiology across all three sources. That makes the Insights tab the dashboard's real centre, and its credibility depends on refusing to overclaim. Sample-size gates, noise bands and "inconclusive" results make it trustworthy, and the two-week experiment loop turns it from a report into a lever. The research shifts what "improving numbers" should mean. The dashboard should reward planned behaviours sustained over weeks, not daily score maxima. Forgiving weekly goals and baseline-relative framing produce more improvement and less anxiety than exact daily targets and broken streaks.

Some uncertainty remains. No controlled study compares rings with bars for goal progress, or calorie ranges with exact targets. The optimal number of headline items is a heuristic. The causal-bias and sham-feedback effects come from small or specific samples. Treat the recommendations as well-grounded defaults, and validate them on this user. The dashboard's own experiment machinery can do that: run the redesign itself as a two-week trial and see whether logged consistency rises.
